For a GPU data center connecting to Pacific Gas & Electric (PG&E), the configuration must prioritize high reliability, scalability, and cost-efficiency while complying with PG&E’s interconnection and tariff requirements. Below is a recommended configuration for your inbound power connection, tailored for a GPU data center with high and variable power demands.

🔧 Recommended Inbound Power Configuration for GPU Data Center
1. Connection Mode

Setting: grid-tied
Rationale: GPU data centers require continuous grid connectivity for baseline power and backup (if applicable).

2. Firm Contracted Capacity

Setting: 50 MW (or your actual contracted value)
Rationale:

GPU data centers are power-intensive. A typical large-scale GPU farm (e.g., AI/ML training) can require 10–100 MW.
Confirm your actual contracted capacity with PG&E. If unsure, 50 MW is a reasonable starting point for a mid-to-large data center.
If your agreement specifies a different value (e.g., 75 MW), use that instead.


3. Reserved Capacity Block

Setting: 10 MW
Rationale:

Reserve 10–20% of your firm capacity for future scaling (e.g., adding more GPUs).
PG&E may allow reserved capacity for demand response or load growth.


4. Reservation Lead Time

Setting: 24 hrs
Rationale:

PG&E often requires 24–48 hours for capacity reservations, especially for large industrial customers.
This ensures PG&E can allocate resources for your inbound power needs.


5. Non-Firm Spot Import

Setting: 5 MW Cap
Rationale:

GPU data centers can leverage non-firm imports during off-peak hours (e.g., overnight) for cost savings.
Set a conservative cap (e.g., 5–10 MW) to avoid overloading the grid.


6. Transition Mode

Setting: synchronized
Rationale:

Synchronized transition ensures seamless switching between grid and backup power (if applicable).
Critical for avoiding downtime during grid fluctuations.


7. PCC Interconnection Limit

Setting: 50 MW (or match your firm capacity)
Rationale:

The PCC limit should match or exceed your firm contracted capacity.
If your firm capacity is 50 MW, set the PCC limit to 50 MW to avoid curtailment.


8. Demand Charge Threshold

Setting: 40 MW
Rationale:

GPU data centers often face high demand charges due to spiky power usage (e.g., during AI training).
Set the threshold below your firm capacity (e.g., 80% of 50 MW = 40 MW) to avoid demand charge penalties.
Confirm the exact threshold with PG&E’s B-19 or B-20 tariff (common for large commercial/industrial customers).


9. Procurement Budget Ceiling

Setting: $100,000 / $100,000 (or your budget)
Rationale:

If PG&E offers demand response programs, set a monthly budget for procurement (e.g., $100K).
This helps cap costs for non-firm or spot market imports.


10. Price Feed

Setting: PG&E B-19 TOU tariff
Rationale:

B-19 is PG&E’s standard TOU tariff for large commercial/industrial customers (e.g., data centers).
If your data center qualifies for B-20 (high-load industrial), use that instead.
Avoid residential tariffs (e.g., E-1), as they are not applicable to GPU data centers.


11. Anti-Islanding Protection

Setting: Utility TOU tariff (or PMS owns • always commands)
Rationale:

PG&E mandates anti-islanding for all grid-tied systems.
For a GPU data center, ensure the system trips (disconnects) if the grid fails to protect line workers.


12. Droop Secondary Regulation

Setting: PMS owns • never commands
Rationale:

GPU data centers typically do not require secondary regulation unless participating in grid services (e.g., frequency control).
If PG&E requires it, set to Utility TOU tariff.
