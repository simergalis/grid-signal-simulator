Focusing on that specifically — here's what the two frames say about it.

The spike is one job arriving at full draw

The compute tile carries the same subtitle in both frames: 1 job · 19.96 MW at full draw. In image 2 that job is drawing 1.80 MW; in image 1 it's drawing 19.96 MW. Same job, same declared full-draw figure, load snapping from baseline to nameplate.

So this isn't an unexplained transient. It's the scenario's injected step-load event — the thing the demo is built around. The question is why it arrives as a wall rather than a ramp, and there are two candidate mechanisms that need different fixes.

Mechanism A — the job power model is binary

The likely implementation is something like compute_load = idle_baseline + sum(job.full_draw for job in running). When the job's state flips to running on a tick, the sum jumps by the whole 19.96 MW at once. There's no job.current_draw interpolating from zero over a warmup window.

Real distributed training jobs don't do this. Process launch, NCCL/collective init, dataloader spin-up, and the first few steps at partial utilization spread the ramp over roughly 10–60 seconds. A 20 MW job ramping over 30 s is ~0.67 MW/s — a rate a spinning turbine can partly follow. A step is a rate no generator can follow.

Mechanism B — you're sampling a ramp at 1/35th of its rate

The readiness counter goes 35 s → 0 s across one second of wall clock, so sim time is running roughly 35× real time. If the load ramp takes 35 sim-seconds, you'd capture it in about two frames — and it would look exactly like a step even if the underlying model ramps correctly.

How to tell them apart: dump compute_load_mw at sim-tick resolution across the event. If there are intermediate values between 1.80 and 19.96, it's Mechanism B and the model is fine — the display is just aliasing it. If it goes 1.80 → 19.96 in a single sim tick, it's Mechanism A. This is a five-minute check and it decides whether you're fixing the load model or the visualization.

Why it matters more than the spike itself

Regardless of mechanism, the magnitude is the problem. The step is 11.1× the pre-step site load, and 19.96 MW happens to equal the plant's entire declared N-1 figure of 20.0 MW. One job consumes the whole site.

Here's who actually covered the +22.89 MW site increase:

Source	Change	Share
BESS (charge → discharge)	+18.92 MW	83%
Gas turbine	+3.67 MW	16%
Solar	+0.30 MW	1%

The turbine — the asset that was supposedly staged 35 seconds ahead — delivered 16% of the step. The battery, which the step-load panel describes as "BESS standby," delivered 83%. Since the battery is also acting as the balance slack variable, it didn't cover the step by decision; it covered it by arithmetic.

That inverts the pitch. The demo currently shows a system where advance warning bought almost nothing and the battery absorbed the event reactively.

There's also a lead-time realism issue worth separating out: 35 seconds only lets you ramp an already-synchronized unit. It's nowhere near enough to start a cold one — even fast-start aeroderivatives need minutes. With 1 of 5 units online, the ceiling on what 35 s of warning can deliver is however far that single unit can ramp, which caps the demonstrable benefit no matter how good the forecast is.

What I'd change

Sizing first: make the step a meaningful fraction of site load rather than a multiple of it — something like 20–40%, which is where real cluster steps land and where pre-staging visibly wins. An 11× step is unfollowable by construction, so it can only ever demonstrate failure.

Then give jobs a ramp profile, so the forecast has a trajectory to track rather than a binary hit-or-miss, and so the turbine's head start converts into actual megawatts. Right now, even a perfect 35-second forecast can't be distinguished from no forecast at all, because the load discontinuity leaves nothing for the generator to do.
