2. Tile-by-Tile Review (before pressing play)
Walk the static screen top-to-bottom. Keep this brief — 30–60 seconds per zone — since the live run will re-surface most of it in context.
Header bar
●	Site identity:  Riverbend DC-West, islanded microgrid, supervised tier. Call out “islanded” and “supervised” deliberately — islanded means there's no utility feed to fall back on, so every megawatt has to come from on-site assets, which is the hardest version of this problem. “Supervised” tier means GridSignal is advising and an operator is in the loop acknowledging actions — not fully autonomous yet. That's an honest maturity signal, not a hedge.
●	LIVE indicator + UTC clock:  This is a running simulation clock, not a replay. Worth a one-line mention so the investor knows the numbers they're about to see are computed in real time, not pre-baked.
Top status strip
●	Run in progress banner (“20s to full load — response already staged”): This is the headline metric of the entire product. Read it exactly as written — the system has already staged turbine and battery response to a load event that hasn't landed yet. Everything below is detail on how.
●	Data centre / location:  Grounds the scenario in a real site profile (San Diego climate, UTC-7) — relevant because thermal lag and solar output are climate-dependent, and the model is using site-specific parameters, not generic defaults.
●	Site Demand / Served / Unserved:  Unserved is the number that matters most to this audience. Zero unserved demand, sustained through a step-load event, is the entire value proposition in one field. Point at it now and tell them to watch it stay at zero as the load ramps.
●	Predicted Peak:  This is the forecast output itself — the number the rest of the system is staging against.
●	Gen-trip cover:  N-1 contingency coverage — can the fleet absorb the loss of its largest online unit without shedding load. This is the language your operator audience already uses for generator adequacy; it's deliberately framed in their vocabulary, not ours.
●	Reserve: sufficient:  The go/no-go verdict. When this flips to insufficient, the system raises it before the event, not after — that's the point to make if asked “what happens when you're wrong.” In today's run, watch for this to flip during the overload phase — flag that in advance: “this is going to alarm partway through, and that's correct behavior, not a failure. It's telling us generation alone can't cover this load — watch what happens to service anyway.” Don't let an unexplained alarm read as something going wrong live.
Plant column (left)
●	Gas Turbine Fleet:  Unit count, online/standby/cold status. This is the slow, expensive, and physically constrained asset — the one with the ramp-rate limit that makes prediction valuable in the first place.
●	Solar PV:  Non-dispatchable — an input the system forecasts and works around, not an asset it commands.
●	Battery (BESS):  The fast-response bridge. Currently absorbing/charging in this frame — worth noting batteries aren't just for outages, they're actively cycling to smooth the gap between a step-load landing and turbines catching up.
○	If asked what actually limits bridging time:  two things, and it's worth naming both because people often assume it's just the one — how much power the battery can output right now, and how much usable energy it actually has in reserve. A big inverter on a battery that's been run down can't help; a full battery behind a small inverter can't help either. The system is continuously checking both before it ever tells you the reserve is sufficient. (Keep this conceptual on this slide — the exact reserve-evaluation math is patent-pending and stays out of investor decks until IP review clears it; describe the idea, not the formula.)
●	Grid Connection (greyed out):  Explicitly disabled — this site is islanded, no utility feed. Reinforces that everything served on this dashboard comes from on-site generation alone.
Center narrative panel — “What You Are Watching”
This is annotation, not decoration — it's telling the investor how to read the plant diagram in real time (turbine tiles lighting sequentially, solar's real-world derate off nameplate, battery bridging pattern). Let them skim it themselves rather than reading it aloud; reference it only if they ask “how do I read this diagram.”
Delivery path (center)
●	Switchgear/PMS → Distribution → PDU/RPP → Compute Racks:  This is the physical delivery chain the forecast is protecting. The PMS tile explicitly states “GridSignal advises — never commands protection” — point at that line directly. It's the clearest on-screen proof of the advisory boundary you just described in the intro.
●	Cooling Plant:  Runs on its own lag, separate from the compute step-load — the two are staged on different clocks against the same predicted event, which is why a compute spike doesn't ambush the chiller plant thirty seconds later.
Compute Racks tile (right of center)
Rack draw is the demand signal the whole system is built around. Note the line about gen-trip cover being measured against this — if the fleet can't cover a unit trip, the rack load is what's exposed.
Step-Load Incoming panel
This is the live prediction surfaced as a countdown: seconds until full draw, what's already staged, and — critically — “Nothing required.” That's the operator-facing payoff: the system already has this covered, no action needed from the human. Contrast this with a reactive system, where this panel wouldn't exist at all — you'd just find out when the frequency moved.
Scheduler Feed / AI Summary
Timestamped log of scheduler events (jobs admitted, node counts, forecast draw) narrated in plain language. This is the literal proof that the signal driving everything above is coming from the compute scheduler, not from power telemetry — the log entries are queue events, not sensor readings. Good tile to point at when explaining the lead-time claim concretely.
In this run the feed is fed by three simulated tenants — one on each scheduler stack we support (Slurm, Kubernetes, Ray) — submitting a realistic, irregular mix of job sizes on their own independent schedules, the way three real customers sharing a site actually would. Point out that the feed doesn't move in neat, evenly-spaced steps; it clusters and bursts the way a real queue does. That's deliberate — it's there so this doesn't look like a scripted trace.
Bottom row — system trust tiles
●	Forecast Quality (currently flagged “Attention”): Confidence band status. Worth calling out unprompted — showing a live data-quality flag, rather than a uniformly green dashboard, is a credibility signal. It shows the system knows when its own confidence is degraded and says so.
●	Generation Commitment Controller (“Ready”): The component holding fleet utilization and reserve state, currently idle/holding.
●	Optimisation Agents (“Armed”): Framed exactly as you'd want a technical investor to hear it — “finding patterns a threshold rule can't — dispatch never waits.” Static rules would apply a worst-case response to every event; this is what makes the response proportionate instead.
Run controls (bottom bar)
Scenario: Shaped Load — 5% → 40% → 120% → 40% → 20% of site capacity. Mention what the shape encodes before it starts moving: this isn't a simple ramp to peak and hold — it deliberately overshoots to 120%, well past nominal, then pulls back down. That's the point to make explicit: you're not just watching the system handle a comfortable load, you're watching it handle a genuine overload event and recover.
Site fleet, worth stating up front:  three 10 MW gas turbines (30 MW total), a 5 MW solar array, and a 6 MW / 8 MWh battery starting at 85% charge. Together that's reliably good for roughly 40 MW. The scenario pushes demand to about 48 MW — 8 MW above nameplate — for a twenty-minute window. Naming the numbers before the run starts is what makes the overshoot land as real rather than abstract.
